Overview

The report offers a high-resolution synthesis of efforts to better predict, prepare for, and prevent catastrophic climate risks. It clarifies the state of the field (what exists today, what is needed and already agreed upon, where are the key gaps) across four main pillars and a synthesis:

1Sensemaking

Synthesizes what can currently be sensed, modeled, and inferred about nonlinear climate risks and examines what it takes to translate that knowledge into decision-relevant understanding—without requiring predictive certainty. It maps gaps in early-warning and monitoring infrastructure, traces how disruptions cascade across systems, and diagnoses the "translation failures" that prevent existing information from activating institutional response.

Lead authors: Laurie Laybourn and Theo Cox, Strategic Climate Risks Initiative
2Preparedness & Prevention

Surveys the “action space” by introducing five key clusters of risks and their unique characteristics and mapping them to case studies of potential response and risk reduction interventions. For each intervention family the chapter assesses current readiness, near-term feasibility, what capabilities must be built, and what enabling infrastructure is required over 10–30-year horizons.

Lead authors: Joshua Elliott, Renaissance Philanthropy
3Governance

Examines the institutional design problem at the heart of stabilization and catastrophic risks: who stewards the response when no single entity owns the problem (and how)? Looks at what the governance needed to research, develop, and deliberate on various response options towards catastrophic climate risks looks like, and how governance impacts the other chapters / pillars.

Lead authors: Ted Parson, UCLA School of Law; Jessica Seddon, Yale Jackson School of Global Affairs
4Legitimacy & License

Treats legitimacy as a critical condition for institutional functionality and takes a capabilities-focused approach to describe different pathways for building and maintaining it during different stages of field development. Examines how the field can create permission to research and test preparedness options without implying permission to deploy them.

Lead authors: Elena Kavanagh, Risk Governance Initiative
5Synthesis

Makes connections across takeaways from each chapter and integrates the report towards a shared “state of the field” and also shared agenda for action, at least where there is moderate alignment. It will close with calls to action segmented by stakeholder groups.

The report’s own authors have varied perspectives and areas of disagreement, and the report is being written to show that range of views, and not to establish a single consensus view. It is not a summary of the summit, and we will not run a consensus or sign-on process with attendees.

1. Sensemaking

Before you can respond effectively to catastrophic climate risks, you must first make sense of them. This requires translating noisy signals into decision-relevant understanding, and ensuring this understanding is structured and located in a way which guides action. We are a long way from that point. The decision support tools (DSTs) used to guide strategy and policy in key institutions around the world carry major blind spots around these risks, often stemming from the very features that make them so concerning: probabilities that cannot be reliably bounded and impacts that cross sectoral and territorial boundaries in complex ways, which are potentially vast in their scale and scope.

2. Preparedness & Prevention

Sensemaking alone does not reduce risk; the field also needs a clear picture of what can actually be done, and how ready each option is. This chapter surveys that action space across five clusters of risk, pairing each with case studies of potential response and risk reduction interventions and assessing, for every intervention family, current readiness, near-term feasibility, the capabilities still to be built, and the enabling infrastructure required over 10–30-year horizons.

3. Governance

Examines the institutional design problem at the heart of stabilization and catastrophic risks: who stewards the response when no single entity owns the problem (and how)? Looks at what the governance needed to research, develop, and deliberate on various response options towards catastrophic climate risks looks like, and how governance impacts the other chapters / pillars.

4. Legitimacy & License

Treats legitimacy as a critical condition for institutional functionality and takes a capabilities-focused approach to describe different pathways for building and maintaining it during different stages of field development. Examines how the field can create permission to research and test preparedness options without implying permission to deploy them.

5. Synthesis

Makes connections across takeaways from each chapter and integrates the report towards a shared “state of the field” and also shared agenda for action, at least where there is moderate alignment. It will close with calls to action segmented by stakeholder groups.
