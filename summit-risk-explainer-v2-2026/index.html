A short explainer

Where we stand on stabilization

Where we are

We're heading past 1.5°C.

2°C is slipping too, and every tenth of a degree still matters. Banks already stress-test for 3°C.

Risk 1 of 3

Earth is getting darker.

With less bright ice and fewer bright clouds, the planet reflects less sunlight than it did 20 years ago.

Risk 1 of 3

So warming may be speeding up.

The heat Earth gains each year roughly doubled in about 20 years. Spark Climate explains the science →

Where we are

Together, they could make this look optimistic.

If these feedbacks speed up, today's path runs hotter still.

Risk 2 of 3

A warmer planet starts warming itself.

Thawing permafrost, wildfires and warming wetlands release carbon and methane, called warming-induced emissions. Permafrost alone holds about twice the carbon in the air.

Risk 3 of 3

One glacier could set off meters of sea-level rise.

Warm ocean water is melting Thwaites Glacier from below. It holds about 65 cm of sea level, and losing it could destabilize much of West Antarctica: about 3 m, over centuries.

Where we are

Big systems could break down.

The Arctic, ice sheets, ecosystems, the planet's brightness. Past certain points, some keep changing even if emissions fall. Three risks show how.

The gap

Picture climate impacts as a curve.

This is the climate of a century ago. Most impacts fell left of the line, where warnings, evacuations and insurance absorb the losses.

The gap

Warming pushes it up and to the right.

Severe impacts get more common, and a tail of catastrophic ones appears.

The gap

Cutting emissions and removing carbon pull it back.

We need far more of both. On their own, they can't pull it back far enough.

The gap

Adapting moves the line.

Better warnings, defenses and insurance let society absorb more.

The gap

But a tail is left.

Even done well, some warming is locked in and tipping points are uncertain. A tail of catastrophic impacts remains.

The gap

Today, we pay for it afterward.

The UN calls this tail “loss and damage.” About $400B a year is needed. The fund for it has under $1B pledged.

What we could do

There's another kind of climate work.

Keeping systems like these from tipping while emissions fall. It's called stabilization, and it doesn't replace cutting emissions.

What we could do

It starts with watching.

NASA's CERES satellites have tracked Earth's energy budget for about 25 years. The next instrument launches in 2027. What comes after it is uncertain.

What we could do

Each risk has a research path.

Watch Earth's energy budget. Keep permafrost frozen and pull methane back out. Slow glaciers and block warm water. Shade reefs and thicken Arctic sea ice.

What we could do

El Niño shows us a hotter world early.

Strong El Niño years bring record heat and drought. Early modeling suggests brightening clouds off South America as one builds might keep it from peaking. It's untested.

What we could do

If Earth reflects less, could we help it reflect more?

That would be global, and it raises the hardest questions: side effects, who decides, and what happens if it stops. It starts as research.

What we could do

We've seen this before.

In 1992, Al Gore called adaptation “a kind of laziness.” Carbon removal was dismissed as “a fig leaf” for polluters. Both are now essential.

What we could do

Stabilization looks like the next one.

The harder the questions, the sooner the research should start.

The field

The field is small, and maturing.

It runs on a few tens of millions of dollars a year. Credible groups now have rigorous research roadmaps and room to grow.

The field

It's a good moment to fund it.

In October, 250+ people from six continents met in Reykjavík. Governments from Iceland to the EU are moving. Funding hasn't caught up.

The field

What it needs.

Ten times today's funding by 2030: hundreds of millions a year. Then billions a year by 2035 as governments join. Even then, under a quarter of 1% of climate spending.

Who's doing the work
A field taking shape.
