Where we stand on stabilization

Warming may be speeding up, and three risks show how fast things could move. Even if we cut emissions and adapt, a tail of catastrophic risk remains. Here is the research that could help, and the small field ready to grow.

01

Where we are

We're heading past 1.5°C.

Holding warming to 1.5°C is no longer possible. The UN Environment Programme's 2026 report on overshoot puts it plainly: exceeding 1.5°C "is inevitable under current emissions trajectories and policy settings." Two degrees is slipping too, and every tenth of a degree still matters.

Those who price risk are already planning for worse. Banks stress-test their portfolios against a 3°C world.

Big systems could break down.

In a hotter world, some of the systems we depend on stop changing gradually. The summit organized them into four connected areas of risk.

The Arctic and ocean currents: shifts in temperature and ocean circulation, including the Atlantic overturning known as AMOC, reshape rainfall and agriculture. Ice sheets: ice loss threatens coastlines and communities for centuries. Ecosystems: living systems sustain livelihoods and regulate carbon, water and heat. Earth's brightness: changes in reflected sunlight can amplify warming.

They're linked, so trouble in one pushes on the others. And the shocks land on human systems: food, water, migration and institutions. Past certain points, some of these systems keep changing even if emissions fall.

Three risks show how fast things could move, and why they're hard to reverse.

A system doesn't need to have a tipping point to be in serious trouble.
Risk 1 of 3

Earth is getting darker.

Ice and bright clouds bounce sunlight back to space. Open ocean absorbs it. With less bright ice and fewer bright low clouds, Earth now reflects less sunlight than it did twenty years ago, so it absorbs more of the sun's energy.

The result shows up in the planet's energy budget. The gap between the energy coming in and the energy going out, which drives warming, roughly doubled in about twenty years, according to NASA's CERES satellites. Much of that change is Earth reflecting less. If it keeps growing, warming accelerates, and every other risk arrives sooner.

Spark Climate has a detailed explainer of the science.

Risk 2 of 3

A warmer planet starts warming itself.

Warming releases carbon that nature has stored for a long time. Permafrost thaws and decomposes, wildfires burn forests and peat, and warming wetlands give off more methane. Scientists call these warming-induced emissions, and each adds to the warming that set it off.

The stakes are large. Permafrost alone holds roughly 1,500 billion tonnes of carbon, about twice what's in the atmosphere. Cutting fossil fuels slows these emissions but can't switch them off, and most national climate targets don't account for them.

Risk 3 of 3

One glacier could set off meters of sea-level rise.

Thwaites Glacier in West Antarctica is roughly the size of Florida. Warm ocean water is reaching under its floating edge and melting it from below. The bed beneath it deepens inland, so as the glacier retreats, more ice meets the ocean.

Thwaites alone holds about 65 cm of global sea level. It also braces the ice around it, so losing it could destabilize much of the West Antarctic Ice Sheet: about 3 meters, over centuries. No one can yet say how fast that could unfold, which is what makes it a risk to prepare for now. The International Thwaites Glacier Collaboration is studying it.

Together, they could push warming past today's projections.

A darker Earth, a planet that warms itself and retreating ice all point the same way: warming that runs ahead of the scenarios in the chart above. Every other risk would arrive sooner, with less time to prepare.

02

The gap

Picture climate impacts as a curve.

Put every climate impact on one axis, from milder to more severe, and how often each happens on the other. Somewhere along that axis is a line: everything to its left our systems are built to absorb, through early warnings, evacuations, insurance and disaster funds. That doesn't mean there are no losses. It means they're absorbable.

Scroll to step through the curve below, or tap a step.

Cutting emissions and removing carbon pull the whole curve back, and we need far more of both. Adapting moves the line. But even done well, some warming is locked in and tipping points are uncertain. A tail of catastrophic impacts remains.

Today, we pay for the tail afterward.

The UN climate process has a name for that tail: loss and damage, the impacts we can't adapt to. Estimates put the need at about $400 billion a year over the coming decades. The fund created for it in 2022 has about $822 million pledged, in total.

These are lives, homes and cultures, not a line item. A plan with a column for catastrophic losses has chosen to pay later instead of preparing.

03

What we could do

There's another kind of climate work.

Alongside bringing greenhouse gases down and adapting, there's work to keep critical Earth systems, like the ones behind these three risks, from tipping while emissions fall. It's called Earth system stabilization.

It doesn't replace cutting emissions, which remains the solution. These are strategies, not solutions: options to develop before they're needed, something like pandemic preparedness for the climate. Nearly all of it today is research, not deployment.

It starts with watching.

Observation drew the broadest agreement at the summit: you can't manage what you can't see. NASA's CERES satellites have tracked Earth's energy budget for about 25 years. The next instrument launches in 2027. What comes after it is uncertain.

Each risk has a research path.

The responses map onto the same risks. Each would need years of research, field trials and governance before it could be used at all.

  • A darker Earth
    Keep watching Earth's energy budget, and study how clouds and aerosols are changing. CLARO tracks the sulfur pollution that once made clouds brighter. Global ideas for reflecting more sunlight raise the hardest questions; more on those below.
  • Warming-induced emissions
    Keeping permafrost frozen, for example by bringing back large grazers like bison and muskox; the Alaska Future Ecology Institute is planning a 7,000-acre experiment. Spark Climate and EDF are scoping research on these emissions and on removing methane.
  • Ice sheets
    Slowing glaciers at their base, which the Arête Glacier Initiative studies, and seabed curtains that keep warm water away from the ice.
  • Reefs and the Arctic
    Brightening clouds over a reef in a marine heatwave, which Australia's Reef Restoration and Adaptation Program has trialed on the Great Barrier Reef. Thickening Arctic sea ice, which the Arctic Stabilization Initiative and an Ocean Visions road map explore.

El Niño shows us a hotter world early.

Strong El Niño years push global temperatures to new highs and bring drought, floods and coral bleaching. They're a preview of where warming is heading. They may also be a place where a regional intervention could help.

Early modeling suggests that brightening clouds off South America while an El Niño is growing might dampen it or keep it from maturing. Kate Ricke's group at Scripps, UC San Diego, studies this approach (Wan et al. 2026, Science Advances). Three things stand out from one climate model, NCAR's CESM2.

  • A natural analog exists. Smoke from the 2019–20 Australian wildfires brightened clouds in this region and helped set off the multi-year La Niña that began in 2020. Simulated brightening there produces a similar response.
  • Timing matters. In re-runs of the 1997–98 and 2015–16 El Niños, brightening from June to February cut the peak warming of the central Pacific, where El Niño strength is measured (the Niño-3.4 region), by 1.88°C, most of the event's peak warming. Starting in December cut it by only 0.31°C.
  • The trade-offs are real. The effect fades once brightening stops, and a weaker El Niño can bring an earlier, stronger La Niña. Early, short interventions may limit this.

These results come from a single model with known biases in how it represents low clouds, and the authors call for comparisons across many models. The biggest unknown is how much the region's clouds actually brighten, which only new observations can answer. Preliminary, unpublished estimates from the group put the avoided damages from one event at $660–960 billion, against about $5 billion for a season of brightening from merchant ships. The group has scoped a research program to test whether any of this holds up. Ricke presented the work in Outlier's learning series.

If Earth reflects less, could we help it reflect more?

It follows from the darkening Earth. But a global approach raises the hardest questions: side effects, who decides, who pays, and what happens if it ever stops. Those questions are the reason to study it carefully and early, with far more people at the table, and to keep it clearly apart from the regional ideas above. It starts as research.

We've seen this before.

In 1992, Al Gore called a focus on adaptation "a kind of laziness." Today it's a core pillar of climate policy. In the 2000s, carbon removal was dismissed as "a fig leaf" for polluters. By 2018, every IPCC pathway to 1.5°C relied on it. Both were ready when needed because people kept working on them before there was agreement.

Stabilization is dismissed today as a distraction. It looks like the next step in the same pattern, and the harder its questions, the sooner the research should start.

04

The field

The field is small, and maturing.

The core of this work runs on a few tens of millions of dollars a year. Carbon removal looked much like this in 2015, the year Carbon180 launched. It has since grown roughly 20 to 25 times in people.

What's changed is readiness. A growing set of credible organizations now run rigorous research roadmaps, from observation to field trials, and can put more funding to work well.

It's a good moment to fund it.

In October 2026, more than 250 people from six continents met in Reykjavík for the Global Risk and Stabilization Summit, from groups that are rarely in the same room: research, government and policy, philanthropy, finance and risk, industry, and civil society. It was a Climate Emergencies Forum event, hosted by ARC. UCLA's Emmett Institute summed it up in three numbers. Governments are starting to move too, from Iceland's security assessment to the EU and Canada's ocean-observing alliance. Funding hasn't caught up.

  • Fall 2025Iceland declares possible AMOC collapse a national security threat.
  • Spring 2026The risk is raised at the Nordic Council of Ministers.
  • Summer 2026Finland launches a national AMOC assessment.
  • 2026The Netherlands studies the impacts on its sea defenses.
  • September 2026The EU and Canada launch the OceanEye ocean-observing alliance, with about €220M pledged.

What it needs.

The field's goals, set out in Reykjavík: ten times today's funding by 2030, or hundreds of millions a year, with dedicated journals and government programs at scale, then billions a year by 2035 as government programs grow. Even then it would cost under a quarter of 1% of what the world spends on climate, which makes it cheap insurance.
